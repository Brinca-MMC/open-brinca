## Parte I · El punto ciego del crecimiento · [06:46](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4N6I6PXycMw&t=14941s)

**[06:46](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4N6I6PXycMw&t=14941s) Stefano Puntoni:** I'll start with the growth blind spot. We define the growth blind spot as a gap between what managers and executives believe that AI can do, which is typically transformational and is going to really change how the performance of the organization, and on the other hand what they actually do with it. I already hinted at that issue with that skew towards efficiency and productivity. I already talked about how people are pursuing this technology mostly to the service of improving what we are doing today: make what we do faster, cheaper, better in some way. However, that has an intrinsic limit. You can drive cost only down to zero. So this is, we call it the obvious truth, basically. On the website you can find a white paper with the math. But really what we did was to take a simple firm valuation model. So if you are a CEO of a company, maybe one of your key goals is to grow the value of your firm. And so we have a model which has revenues and costs and a multiplier of the earnings. That's how companies typically are valued. And we make a couple of assumptions. You'll be the judge of whether those assumptions make sense, but actually you can tweak those assumptions, and the key results are pretty robust. So we argue that, let's assume that half of all a company's cost could be affected by AI. AI is not going to change your electricity bill, it's not going to change your rent, but it might change other things that you might be able to save some money on. So imagine then that for the 50% of all your costs that AI can affect, we are going to have a reduction of 10% on all those costs across the entire organization. So it's quite an ambitious assumption, but let's assume that we can do that. Then if you do the math, basically after three years that will lead to an increase in the value of your company of 10%. Not nothing, 10% is 10%, but it's not transformational. It's just 10%.

**[09:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4N6I6PXycMw&t=15075s)** On the other hand, imagine that I do something else. I don't really focus everything on cost reduction. Instead, I'm going to focus on growth. Let's make more assumptions. Let's assume that your organic growth rate is 3%, and let's assume that you invest in AI tools and systems to boost your ability to grow your business by finding new customers, upselling those customers, cross-selling those customers and so on, and let's assume that you are able to push that up from 3 to 5%. So quite a big increase, but not a crazy increase. Within three years, just doing that will increase the value of your firm by 50%. And even more, imagine that you do a little bit of both. You do a little bit of productivity and you do growth. Or reallocate resources efficiently to boost the power to grow. And maybe by doing that, for example, you can get salespeople, maybe they can do what they're doing in 80% of the time. And instead of firing 20% of the salespeople, you get them to make four more calls. You reallocate the effort, so they can spend more time on growth initiatives. Let's assume that you are able to boost that growth rate to 7%, another 2%, by reallocating effort that way. I don't know if it's possible, but let's imagine. If you could do that, within three years your value of the firm will have more than doubled. So basically the point I'm making here is a simple one, which is that the upside potential of growth on the impact on firm value is huge, while the potential of cost reduction is much more limited. So if you care about the value of your firm, you've got to pursue an AI for growth agenda. That's basically the point here.

**[10:46](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4N6I6PXycMw&t=15181s)** Now, some data that seem to bear this out. This is a new survey from PwC. They surveyed over 1,000 executives, and there are a lot of questions about AI programs and outcomes. What they find is that 75% of the economic value generated by AI is generated by only 20% of the firms. And what is different about those 20%? Well, those 20% are the firms that are using AI for growth and innovation. So it seems to be consistent with that point that I made. And here's from our results, from our survey again. These are just fresh off the press, we're still analyzing the data, but I wanted to share this result. We have one question about ROI, and we have quite a large number of people in the sample that report that AI is already showing quite positive ROI. And if you select the number of people who say it's giving strongly positive ROI, so the most positive answer on the scale, and you look at who it is that, you know, says that... We basically have another measure that taps onto what they measure when it comes to AI. And we divide firms between those that track AI performance in terms of growth measures and those that track AI performance only in terms of efficiency measures. And what we find is that over a third of the companies that say that they track growth report strongly positive ROI, while instead only less than 20% of those that track only efficiency report a very strong ROI. Again, correlation is not causation, this is just a data point, but I think it's suggestive of what I was arguing: that basically the road to success with AI is not efficiency and productivity, but actually is growth and innovation.

## De subir colinas a escalar montañas: el motor de descubrimiento conductual · [12:42](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4N6I6PXycMw&t=15297s)

**[12:42](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4N6I6PXycMw&t=15297s) Stefano Puntoni:** I took the advantage of the opportunity of talking about hills and mountains to show you a beautiful picture of the mountains of Chile. And so the argument that I make is that we really should change mindset from climbing hills to climbing mountains. And I'm going to show you how one might be doing that. But just to illustrate that idea, this is just a schematic illustration of the notion of this mindset shift. So you have an idea space that you can explore to do things better. Imagine in marketing: you might run a campaign, you might do emails, whatever it might be, some programs that you implement in order to grow. And typically our approach is this A/B testing kind of approach. For example, on the website you would change the picture, or you would change a headline of an ad, or whatever, the title of an email, whatever it might be, and you try to optimize all of that through A/B testing. And so you have a performance, which is the y-axis, and you have the idea space, metaphorically, which is the x-axis.

**[13:48](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4N6I6PXycMw&t=15363s)** And people tend to basically optimize on the initiative that they're running. So now they have the green dot, that is a campaign you used to do last month, say. Now you run an A/B test, you try some variants of that, make it better. You find that one doesn't really do better, maybe does worse, but one maybe is a little bit better. And then you made an improvement, and you run with that. So we are optimizing all kinds of decisions in that way. However, I argue what we mostly do is to climb a little hill. And maybe there's a much bigger hill somewhere else, which we haven't even looked at. And the reason why is that we don't have the opportunity to explore the idea space extensively enough. We are too narrow in our testing. Imagine if AI was enabling you to test much more ideas across that idea space. Maybe you'll find a much bigger hill out there that enable you to do much better by doing something quite different. This is the idea of a discovery engine, as we call it. So I want to describe that in some detail, more concretely.

**[14:53](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4N6I6PXycMw&t=15428s)** What is a behavioral discovery engine? It's a system that basically iterates in this way. You start with a business objective, which should be growth-related. It could be finding new customers, it could be upselling your customers, it could be a referral, it could be whatever it might be. And now you have a virtual lab. The virtual lab is made of two main components: virtual scientists and virtual audiences. The virtual scientists are large language models that have been trained on behavioral science literature and results to generate interventions and ideas about how to optimize that business objective that you've got. And so in the A/B test we might try one idea or two ideas, but maybe the virtual scientists can generate 500 ideas. The problem is you can never test 500 ideas in the field. It's impossible. Even the largest company would not be able to do that. So what do we do instead? Well, maybe we can leverage AI again and create a virtual audience. These are digital twins of real customers. They are simulations of customers.

**[16:03](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4N6I6PXycMw&t=15498s)** Actually, there's a wonderful example just outside here that I really recommend you to try it out, put together by our friends at Brinca, where you can simulate an employee base and see how they respond to your messages about AI. It's a very neat idea, and I think we apply it not in the context of employees, but we apply it to the context of customers. We basically get data from the company. It could be aggregate data, it could be individual level data, depending a little bit. And basically, we can simulate an audience that way. We can pilot test it and make sure that on historical data the digital twins perform as they should, that their click-through rates, for example, or whatever, are kind of in the ballpark of the real ones. And once you think you've got a reasonably well calibrated digital twin population, you can now run hundreds of interventions on that audience. They never get tired. They never say no. They always pay attention. They always respond. And so you can test many, many things. More than that, you can even ask for feedback: what is it about this ad or message or email or whatever that you did like or you did not like? And that feedback can be given back to the virtual scientist, who then can iterate and produce new interventions based on the feedback, which may be better next time. You iterate to improve, and at some point that loop of continuous learning reaches its end, and then you think you've done the best you can do, and then you go out and field test those ideas for real. But you only test one at that point, because you've done all the testing in the lab.

**[17:31](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4N6I6PXycMw&t=15586s)** I'm going to show you, there is also one study that we did recently with a major American life insurance company. They were interested in converting term client into permanent client. These are people who bought a life insurance for a period, and they wanted to make it permanent. And so they send them an email campaign. This is basically an email effort. What we did was to test 36 different variants, so not 100 in this case, but 36. Many more than you would test in a normal kind of testing environment. And we created 30,000 digital twins of the customers of this life insurance by using data that they gave us about the segmentation, the personas and things like that. We did the whole process in six weeks, end to end. We did it in two rounds. In round one, we created 18 variants using the virtual scientists. These were basically all founded upon big ideas in behavioral science, like social proof or loss aversion, or ideas of that sort. The emails did quite okay: they did better than the baseline, but not very much better.

**[18:41](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4N6I6PXycMw&t=15656s)** We asked the digital twins to tell us why, and what we heard from the twins was that the messages were perceived as too pushy, too salesy: they're trying to sell this life insurance, I don't want it. That feedback was given back to the virtual scientist, and the virtual scientists then created 18 new versions, which we also tested on the virtual audience. And the winner campaign in that virtual test was one where the virtual scientists took that feedback that the customers didn't want a pushy message and created a message that was not pushing at all. In fact, it was not persuasion in any sense. It was simply saying: check your policy details. You know, verify your coverage details. An invitation to check, you know, what were they covered for. Not to tell them you should get this, not to buy that. Just check it, okay?

**[19:42](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4N6I6PXycMw&t=15717s)** We call this a verification nudge. We did not see it in the literature. This is not something we had trained the virtual scientists to do. Nobody in the team had proposed this. This came straight from the AI system. But that seemed to be the one that performed best on the digital twins. So what we did, together with the company, we actually ran a field experiment where we checked their baseline campaign, the incumbent campaign, which they had been fine-tuning and improving over years, so this is the best that they could do, against this new verification nudge that the AI had come up with. And what we found was a lift of almost 80% in the quote request, the click-through rate from that invitation email. Now we are waiting to get the data of knowing how many of that converted into customers, but I think it's very promising. It's a very big lift for a simple study. Six weeks, all done within that virtual lab.

**[20:38](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4N6I6PXycMw&t=15773s)** We ran another study with wealth management firms, this time with a LinkedIn advertising campaign, and in the virtual lab the best-performing copy was performing around the 3x of the baseline, and in the field we realized a 3.2x. Again, it seemed to be quite accurate. You don't use this technology to get the perfect point estimate. You want to get a direction. You want to get a sense: this is better than that. I don't think you believe that this is 35% better than that or something, but it give you a sense of where you should be going. So this is what the external part, the marketing piece: how AI could be a tool for growth. And if you pursue growth with AI, you're going to stand to reap much bigger benefit than if you focus only on efficiency. Again, there's nothing wrong with productivity. If you can be productive, of course, by all means, be productive. What I'm saying is that a relentless and unique focus on productivity is crowding out other things that are actually more important.

## Estrategias de afrontamiento y el marco AWARE · [26:49](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4N6I6PXycMw&t=16144s)

**[26:49](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4N6I6PXycMw&t=16144s) Stefano Puntoni:** And what we argue is that if people feel threatened, they will cope with that threat in some way. And those ways are actually fairly predictable. So we examine the literature and we sketch what are the main coping strategies that people will engage in if they feel threatened by AI. The most obvious, we call it direct resolution. And that is a good coping strategy, adaptive. It makes you stronger in that era of AI. Basically, you tackle the threat directly. You build AI skills, you become an effective complement to that technology, so that you can actually thrive in the new workplace driven by AI. I'm actually excited about a new project that my research group at the Wharton School is conducting together with Brinca and one of their clients, where we're trying to monitor over time the impact of a company-wide training program. I'd like to see how providing support, both psychological and technical, to all employees in a company is going to boost confidence, boost usage, boost performance and boost well-being. That's the hope. I'm very excited to look at the results when we get them after the end of the training.

**[28:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4N6I6PXycMw&t=16215s)** Now, this is direct resolution. There's another one that is adaptive, which we call fluid compensation. And this is more about pivoting. If you see that one part of your job is going to be largely automated, what you can do is to redirect your focus into that part of the job that is not going to be automated and get as good as you can at that. So take, for example, a content marketing manager writing content marketing things for a B2B company. Now, a lot of content that I think now is done by AI, and the person might feel: what am I going to do? Well, the strategy piece behind the content might not be automated, and maybe you can pivot and focus more on the strategy piece and less on the writing itself. Now, unfortunately, there are also coping strategies that are not positive, that are not adaptive. They are maladaptive. And the two most obvious ones are dissociation and escapism. Dissociation is about AI avoidance: you don't want to use it. And even AI sabotage: I want it to fail. So, for example, they give me access to an AI software, and I use it in a way that is kind of bad, and I show that it gives bad results, and I use that as an excuse to say no, I can't use this because it's too bad. Or escapism, which is: you stop thinking, you basically become a copy and paste machine, you take the output and you spend all day just browsing social media. And basically that's obviously not good for the employees, and it's also not good for the company. So what you want to do as a leader is to increase the likelihood that people are going to have adaptive coping strategies and minimize the likelihood of maladaptive coping strategies.

**[29:35](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4N6I6PXycMw&t=16310s)** This is what an efficiency-only narrative can buy you. These are data from surveys done online by different companies; these are not my data. We reviewed this in a Harvard Business Review article we published a few months ago. There's about a third of knowledge workers in the US that admit to sabotaging their AI strategy. Over 40% of Gen Z workers. Now, if about a third of your employees are trying to make a program fail, that program will fail. There's nothing you can do about that. So that's obviously a concern. Over a majority of the people say that they're using unapproved tools in secret, so-called shadow AI. This creates all kinds of liabilities and risks. And there's about a third who say that they hide AI usage from their employers, because they don't want to know them that they use it, because they want to keep it either as a secret advantage, or they are afraid that if they learn how much AI they're using, they'll be fired or given more work or be penalized in some way. So this is obviously not great. People are going to cope, and if the coping is maladaptive, it will be underground, and you will suffer the consequences of that, maybe without even knowing it is happening.

**[30:47](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4N6I6PXycMw&t=16382s)** Instead, if you take a growth focus, what this is doing is that it's enlisting employees for the benefit of a shared objective. It makes them partners in this AI transformation, and it doesn't make them a victim of the AI transformation. So they are much more likely to go along with the AI agenda. They're much more likely to bring their own expertise and capabilities to making AI a success. And as a result of that, I also believe that these AI programs are much more likely to succeed. We propose in that Harvard Business Review article that I mentioned earlier a framework which we call AWARE, which is a set of things that the leaders ought to be paying attention to. What we argue is that any time you do an AI deployment program, you should always have two tracks. You should have the technical track, where you work with engineers and data scientists and compliance people, the tools, the vendors, to make sure that the AI works. And then you need another parallel track, which is a people track: understand how they react, how that innovation is interfering with their competences, with their autonomy, with their relatedness, and bring it to them in a way that they feel part of that solution instead of becoming part of the problem.

**[32:04](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4N6I6PXycMw&t=16459s)** And so you start by acknowledging how AI might affect well-being. Be transparent, be honest, and ask them that they should tell you whether this feels scary, or if they're concerned about jobs or careers or things like that. Then watch for maladaptive coping. What are the signs that people might be disengaging, might be escaping? And if that is the case, then what can you do to prevent that maladaptive coping? You can align training, job design and feedback with the workers to emphasize as much as possible those adaptive coping. For example, make budgets available for retraining. Give them an opportunity to engage in direct resolution or fluid compensation, so that they can turn that threat into something good for them and not something bad. Then you want to redesign workflows end to end to work towards complementarity. It shouldn't be human or AI. It should be human and AI. Your job as leaders is not to make human intelligence replaced and obsolete. It's to optimize the collective intelligence of the organization. Intelligence from silicon, but also intelligence from carbon, both of them. And finally, empower workers to have a voice, involved in those AI programs. People in the function that actually are deploying AI, these are the real experts on how you get that job done. So you really need their voice and their involvement to make sure that AI is used in the best possible way. Also as a complement to their own expertise and effort.
